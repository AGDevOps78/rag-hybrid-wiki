This is related to the ideal of using Gibb's equimolar quantities rather than continuum numbers such as area, that is keeping the number of surface atoms constant. In this case the surface stress is defined as the derivative of the surface energy with strain, that is (deliberately using a different symbol) This second definition is more convenient in many cases.[6] A conventional liquid cannot sustain strains,[2] so in the continuum definition the surface stress and surface energies are the same, whereas in the atomistic approach the surface stress is zero for a liquid. So long as care is taken[6] the choice of the two does not matter, although this has been a little contentious in the literature.[7][8][9] The origin of surface stress is the difference between bonding in the bulk and at a surface. The bulk spacings set the values of the in-plane surface spacings, and consequently the in-plane distance between atoms. However, the atoms at the surface have a different bonding, so would prefer to be at a different spacing, often (but not always) closer together. If they want to be closer, then d γ / d e i j {\displaystyle d\gamma /de_{ij}} will be positive—a tensile or expansive strain will increase the surface energy. For many metals the derivative is positive, but in other cases it is negative, for instance solid argon and some semiconductors.
