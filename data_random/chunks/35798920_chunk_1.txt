Likewise, unmarried women did not lack the support of their community.[4] People married outside of their immediate clan groups, and this allowed bands to forge relationships with one another.[5] As societies began to develop larger surpluses, and began to settle into more agrarian, sedentary structures, marriage became more important.[6] Wealthier families became less interested in sharing resources, and instead began to use marriage as a tool to consolidate those resources within the family or between a few closely linked kin groups.[7] Studies of genetic material have shown that endogamy became increasingly common among wealthier families. As wealthy families accumulated more and more resources, and people were subject to more taxation, inheritance rights became of greater concern, and marriage came to play an important role in many ancient societies. Marriage became one of the best ways for people to climb the income ladder and recruit economic partners. Both men and women came under more pressure from families to choose the "correct" spouse for the family's interests, and the distinction between "legitimate" and "illegitimate" children grew sharper. The earliest written records show that by the time writing was invented, marriage became the way most wealth and land changed hands. Nevertheless, marriages were typically private affairs and ancient governments rarely got involved.[3] Because women had the unique ability to bear a child with an "impure" bloodline, generally their sexual behavior was more strictly supervised, especially among the aristocracy.[8] Some societies developed elaborate ideas about female sexual purity. Even among the working class, children were essential to help maintain farms and family businesses, so infertile women were often cast aside.[3] The invention of the plow widened the division of labor between men and women. Plowing required greater physical strength and was less compatible with childcare, and therefore became associated with male labor.
