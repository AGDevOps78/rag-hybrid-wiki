The bride's family then countered with a dowry, which separated her from a common concubine. Once all the goods were exchanged, the bride was taken to the groom's ancestral home, where she was expected to obey her husband and live with his relatives. Women continued to belong to their husband's families even after their husbands died; if they had any children they stayed with his family. If the widow's birth family wanted her to marry again, they would often have to ransom her back from her deceased husband's family.[44] According to Hindu tradition, marrying was a holy act, and an unmarried person was considered impious. In some cases, unmarried people were ineligible to participate in religious ceremonies.[3] It was thought that one's daughter is only temporary, and that she was meant to be her husband's. The main duty of a girl's parents was to arrange her marriage and provide a dowry (an insufficient dowry could be seen as an insult). After marriage, a woman became part of her husband's home, and was a guest in her birth family's home.
