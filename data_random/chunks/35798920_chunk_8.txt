In-laws frequently fought over inheritance rights. To curb this, early pharoahs sometimes married their sisters or half-sisters; the heir's legitimacy was then perceived as essentially doubled. Other pharoahs purposely married the female relatives of previous rulers, proclaimed they were married to gods. In the eighth century BCE, one pharoah revived an older practice of the "Divine Wife" -- elevating a pharaoh's daughter to become a priestess committed to celibacy. Although it was possible to divorce, it was difficult. Men often divorced their wives if they were unable to reproduce. After about 365 B.C, divorce laws strongly favored women. A first, marriages in ancient Egypt were usually exclusive, but it also was not uncommon for a man of high economic status to have more than one wife, especially if his first wife was unable to have children of her own.
