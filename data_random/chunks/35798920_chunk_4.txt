Women had control over most of their property and could serve as legal persons who brought cases to the court.[11] Men who married higher-status women often were forced to yield to significant pressure from their wives and fathers-in-law.[14] Even pharaohs frequently found themselves under control over their mothers.[10] Widows gained more legal freedom after their husbands' deaths -- notably, the ability to buy and sell land.[15] Marriage for pharaohs was a more politically treacherous affair. Pharoahs often had multiple wives, and upper-class men would often marry off their female relatives to pharoahs to solidify connections with the royal family and represent their interests.[14] In-laws frequently fought over inheritance rights. To curb this, early pharoahs sometimes married their sisters or half-sisters; the heir's legitimacy was then perceived as essentially doubled.[16] Other pharoahs purposely married the female relatives of previous rulers, proclaimed they were married to gods. In the eighth century BCE, one pharoah revived an older practice of the "Divine Wife" -- elevating a pharaoh's daughter to become a priestess committed to celibacy.[3] Although it was possible to divorce, it was difficult. Men often divorced their wives if they were unable to reproduce.[17] After about 365 B.C, divorce laws strongly favored women.[11] A first, marriages in ancient Egypt were usually exclusive, but it also was not uncommon for a man of high economic status to have more than one wife, especially if his first wife was unable to have children of her own.[11] After Alexander of Macedonia's empire broke apart late in the fourth century BCE, during the Seleucid and Ptolemaic dynasties, polygamy became more widespread in an effort to help kings establish more alliances.[16] Among ancient Germanic tribes, the bride and groom were roughly the same age and generally older than their Roman counterparts, at least according to Tacitus: The youths partake late of the pleasures of love, and hence pass the age of puberty unexhausted: nor are the virgins hurried into marriage; the same maturity, the same full growth is required: the sexes unite equally matched and robust, and the children inherit the vigor of their parents.[18] Where Aristotle had set the prime of life at 37 years for men and 18 for women, the Visigothic Code of law in the 7th century placed the prime of life at 20 years for both men and women, after which both presumably married. Tacitus states that ancient Germanic brides were on average about 20 and were roughly the same age as their husbands.[19] Tacitus, however, had never visited the German-speaking lands and most of his information on Germania comes from secondary sources. In addition, Anglo-Saxon women, like those of other Germanic tribes, are marked as women from the age of 12 and older, based on archaeological finds, implying that the age of marriage coincided with puberty.[20] In the early years of ancient Greece, family ties were the primary way in which social life was organized. Marriages, likewise, required no state intervention – only mutual agreement and the fact that the couple must regard each other as husband and wife accordingly.[21] As Athenian reformers pushed for more robust government to prevent families from gaining too much power (as they were in city-states outside of Athens), they began to enact laws governing everyday life -- including marriage.
