A review of 18 studies found that intersex people were more likely to have depressive and anxiety disorders.[78] A survey of intersex adults in the U.S. found that 61.1% of respondents reported having depressive disorders compared to 19% of the general adult population. Post-traumatic stress disorder was also reported by 40.9% of those surveyed.[79] Surgeries on intersex people as infants can be a cause of mental trauma. Scarring, infertility, and other health issues caused by such surgeries can result in shame, gender dysphoria, sexual dysfunction and feeling betrayal or devalued.[80] Stressful diagnosis procedures and medical treatments can feel like a challenge to one's gender identity, contributing to psychological distress and stigmatization.[78] Intersex students are often the target of abuse at school, especially in bathrooms and changing rooms. Students whose appearance do not fit expected gender norms are at even greater risk of mistreatment.[81] A survey in Mexico found that 26% of intersex respondents reported being bullied as children compared to about 15 percent of endosex respondents.[82] Intersex students are often told to keep medical treatments secret by family members or clinicians, leading to further isolation and untreated trauma.[81] Intersex people are at an increased risk of suicide; in a European study, 6.8% of intersex people age 16 or older reporting having attempted suicide compared to 1.8% of the general population sampled.[83] Intersex people are more likely to experience suicidal thoughts as well.[78] In a Mexican survey, 16% of intersex respondents reported experiencing suicidal ideation compared to 10% of endosex women and 7% of endosex men.[82] Intersex people can face issues that worsen their mental health such as stigma and discrimination, bullying, family rejection, tension with partners about fertility, etc.[84][75] It is not uncommon for intersex people born with ambiguous sex characteristics to experience gender dysphoria. A Those with intersex variations should have a psychiatrist or psychologist working with a multidisciplinary team.[86] Mental health professionals can aid intersex people when making choices about hormone treatments, gender assignment, gender reassignment, and surgeries.[86] Psychotherapy can also be used to support self-acceptance.[87] Psychological support for parents and family members helps reduce harm to an intersex child. This support helps by educating family against misconceptions about intersex variations, as well as encouraging parents to share age-appropriate information with their child.[88] This aids in preventing secrecy about the child's intersex status.[88] Findings suggest peer support for intersex people and their parents can positively contribute to their well-being, though robust evidence is lacking.[84][89] Peer support groups supervised by a facilitator serve to mitigate social isolation, provide emotional validation, and help members of the group process medical information.[89] Accessing peer support can prove difficult due to lack of referrals.[89] Some intersex variations can impact cognitive functioning or carry higher rates of neurodevelopmental conditions like autism or ADHD. Rates of autism symptoms are higher in intersex people.
