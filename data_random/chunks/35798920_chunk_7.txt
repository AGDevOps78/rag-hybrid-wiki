Women had control over most of their property and could serve as legal persons who brought cases to the court. Men who married higher-status women often were forced to yield to significant pressure from their wives and fathers-in-law. Even pharaohs frequently found themselves under control over their mothers. Widows gained more legal freedom after their husbands' deaths -- notably, the ability to buy and sell land. Marriage for pharaohs was a more politically treacherous affair. Pharoahs often had multiple wives, and upper-class men would often marry off their female relatives to pharoahs to solidify connections with the royal family and represent their interests. In-laws frequently fought over inheritance rights. To curb this, early pharoahs sometimes married their sisters or half-sisters; the heir's legitimacy was then perceived as essentially doubled.
