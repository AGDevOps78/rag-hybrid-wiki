Hyper globalists argue that globalization has engulfed today's world in such a way that state boundaries are beginning to lose significance. However, skeptics disregard this as naiveté, believing that the nation-state remains the supreme actor in international relations. [15] George Modelski defines global order as a 'management network centred on a lead unit and contenders for leadership, (pursuing) collective action at the global level'.[16] The system is allegedly cyclical. Each cycle is about 100 years in duration and a new hegemonic power appears each time: Portugal the Netherlands United Kingdom (1) United Kingdom (2) the United States Each cycle has four phases; 1, Global war, which a) involves almost all global powers, b) is 'characteristically naval' [18] c) is caused by a system breakdown, d) is extremely lethal, e) results in a new global leader, capable of tackling global problems.[19] The war is a 'decision process' analogous to a national election.[20] The Thirty Years War, though lasting and destructive, was not a 'global war' [21] 2, World power, which lasts for 'about one generation'.[22] The new incumbent power 'prioritises global problems', mobilises a coalition, and is decisive and innovative.[23] Pre-modern communities become dependent on the hegemonic power [24] 3, Delegitimation. This phase can last for 20–27 years; the hegemonic power falters, as rival powers assert new nationalistic policies.[25] 4, Deconcentration. The hegemony's problem-solving capacity declines. It yields to a multipolar order of warring rivals. Pre-modern communities become less dependent.[26] A challenger appears (successively, Spain, France, France, Germany, and the USSR)[17] and a new global war ensues.
