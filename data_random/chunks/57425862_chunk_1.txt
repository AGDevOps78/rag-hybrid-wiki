Geographic contiguity is important in biology, especially animal ranges. For a particular species, its habitat may be a 'contiguous range', or it might be broken, requiring periodic, typically seasonal migrations (see: Disjunct distribution). The same concept of contiguous range is true for human transportation studies in an attempt to understand census geography. It also comes into play with electoral geography and politics. In United States real property and mineral rights law, touching of two tracts at a common corner (as checkerboarded land) is generally considered contiguous. Regions that are administered as one, but do not form a single integral landmass on the map, are referred to as non-contiguous regions. Examples include Angola, divided into the mainland and the Cabinda exclave to the north, and various non-contiguous transcontinental countries.
