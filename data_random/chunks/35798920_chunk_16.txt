When choosing spouses, families considered economic status and political views. Brides and grooms, and their families, were considered equals.[64] Romance in marriage was discouraged, as it was thought to undermine filial piety. According to one proverb, "Those who come together in passion stay together in tears." Sexual gratification was more acceptable outside of one's marriage, through concubines, geishas, or "pleasure districts".[62][65] During the Heian period (794- High-ranking men sometimes kept multiple wives or concubines, and their children with these concubines were considered just as legitimate. There was language to separate wives from concubines, and distinguish between their respective children, but children of concubines were just as entitled to inheritance as children of wives.[67] The practice of matrilocality in Korea started in the Goguryeo period and ended in the early Joseon period.[68][69] The Korean saying that when a man gets married, he is "entering jangga" (the house of his father-in-law), stems from the Goguryeo period.[70] Both Muslim writers in the Islamic world were more approving of sexual passion between husband and wife than prominent thinkers in Europe; too much intimacy, however, could be an affront to God. Sunni Muslims declared temporary mut'ah marriages unacceptable, though Shiite Muslims continued the practice.[3] Marriage continued to be a primarily economic arrangement in Medieval Europe.
