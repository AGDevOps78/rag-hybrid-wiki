Early maturing girls have been found to be at risk for teenage pregnancy,[35] drinking[36] and weight problems,[37][38] and giving birth to low birth weight infants.[39] Early maturing boys are at risk for sexual promiscuity and delinquency[40] and testicular[41] and prostate cancer.[42] Individual difference in pubertal timing may be influenced by weight, physical activity, and genetics.[43] Menarche, a central event of female puberty, is associated with the father's absence.[44][45][46][47][48][49] According to the evolutionary explanation, an unstable home environment (e.g. father absence) discourages a long-term mating life history, leading girls to adopt a short-term reproductive strategy, such as early menarche.[50] This is because they perceive resources they have as scarce and, possibly, their lifespan to be shorter, under the influence of father absence. Early menarche can increase the chance of fertility, while other short-term reproductive strategies can diversify the genes inherited in offspring. These could lift up a higher success rate of rearing children to adolescence. Moreover, the stress of father absence prompts girls to develop a variety of internalizing disorders, such as bulimia and depression, which may lower the person's metabolism leading to excessive weight gain which precipitates early menarche.[38][51] A study shows that there are fewer monitored meals in the father-absent household.[52] Having meals in the family is arguably more beneficial to children than eating alone (i.e. solitary eating), as the former lowers the chance of obesity. It has been disputed whether the environmental stress of a father's absence stimulates weight gain, and thus accelerates early puberty.[53][54] Likewise, the stress arising from the absence of mother has been shown to have little influence on the child's body weight. Since mother absence does not predict weight gain in children, it seems that the increase in the child's body weight observed is due to the isolated genetic influence of an absent father, rather than the global environmental stress caused by the absence of either parent.[55] This is possible because in ancestral times the survival rate of children with mother being absent was extremely low.
