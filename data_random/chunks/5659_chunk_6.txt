Only 26 elements are monoisotopic, having exactly one stable isotope; these have an odd atomic number of protons, with the exception of beryllium-9 which has an odd number of neutrons.[39][40] The mean number of stable isotopes for the 80 stable elements is 3.1 stable isotopes per element. The largest number of stable isotopes for a single element is 10 (for tin, element 50).[37] Elements with atomic numbers 1 through 82 each have at least one stable isotope (except for technetium, element 43 and promethium, element 61, which have no stable isotopes). Of these 11 transient elements, five (polonium, radon, radium, actinium, and protactinium) are relatively common decay products of thorium and uranium.[48] The remaining six transient elements (technetium, promethium, astatine, francium, neptunium, and plutonium) occur only rarely,[49] as products of rare decay modes or nuclear reaction processes involving uranium or other heavy elements. The remaining 24 heaviest elements (those beyond plutonium, element 94) are radioactive, with half-lives so short that they are not found on Earth and must be synthesized.[50] Five have been discovered in the spectrum of Przybylski's star, from element 95 (americium) to 99 (einsteinium). These are thought to be neutron capture products of uranium and thorium.[51] All 24 heavier elements are radioactive, with short half-lives; if any of these elements were present when the Earth formed, they are certain to have completely decayed, and if present in novae, are in quantities too small to have been noted.
